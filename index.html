Project 04 · Ireland / Energy / Data

How did Ireland change what powers its electricity system?

A 2010–2025 evidence-first analysis of generation mix, operating flexibility, thermal efficiency, imported-fuel exposure and generation economics.

The central finding is not simply that renewables grew. Ireland moved from a system dominated mainly by gas toward one increasingly organised around wind + gas, while imports and storage became more important system resources.

QuestionHow has Ireland’s electricity generation mix changed, and what external fuel and import exposure remains?
Evidence

CSO, SEAI, EirGrid, EU ETS and gas-market evidence.

Outcome

A system-transition view spanning renewables, gas flexibility, imports and cost exposure.

01 · TRANSITION

The scale changed modestly. The composition changed radically.

Domestic generation rose only about 10% between 2010 and 2025. Over the same period, wind expanded more than fourfold, coal collapsed, solar emerged, and gas's share fell while remaining the largest single source.

By 2025, gas + wind supplied roughly 88% of the long-run domestic-generation series.
02 · MIX

From single-fuel dominance to a two-engine system.

The top-two share increased, but the HHI fell because gas no longer dominated as overwhelmingly on its own. Leadership became more evenly split between gas and wind.

03 · OPERATION

Gas matters for flexibility — but the relationship is not one-for-one.

Monthly gas–wind correlation changes materially by year. That is a warning against reducing Irish dispatch to “less wind = more gas”. Demand, imports, storage, outages, hydro, solar and prices all matter.

Pearson monthly correlation. Descriptive only; not causal evidence.

04 · EFFICIENCY

The thermal fleet became smaller, somewhat more efficient — and more gas-heavy.

What changed?

Public thermal conversion efficiency improved from roughly 45.5% in 2010 to 49.5% in 2025.

At the same time, gas rose to roughly 81% of public-thermal fuel input, while coal and peat retreated sharply.

This is an aggregate energy-balance ratio, not the engineering efficiency of every plant.

05 · SECURITY

Domestic electricity does not necessarily mean indigenous energy.

Ireland can generate electricity inside the country while remaining exposed to international fuel markets. Natural gas — the dominant thermal fuel — was still heavily import-dependent in 2025.

This distinction is critical: generation location and energy origin are not the same thing.
06 · ECONOMICS

There is no honest single cost league table.

Gas fuel + carbon cost and renewable RESS strike prices represent different economic concepts. The project therefore models gas as a sensitivity and treats RESS as an Irish investment/support benchmark.

07 · CONCLUSION

Decarbonisation improved. External exposure did not disappear.

Ireland is moving toward a system combining indigenous variable renewables, gas flexibility, interconnection and emerging storage. The policy/economic problem is therefore not simply “which technology is cheapest?” but how to balance decarbonisation, flexibility, investment cost, fuel-price exposure and security of supply.

Ireland Electricity Generation Analytics · Project 04

Project 04 · Final conclusion

What the evidence says about Ireland's electricity transition

What changed?

Between 2010 and 2025, Ireland's long-run domestic generation series increased only from 26.63 TWh to 29.31 TWh. The scale changed modestly; the composition changed radically.

Wind's share rose from 10.5% to 40.1%, while gas fell from 64.4% to 47.5% and coal fell from 13.0% to 1.5%. By 2025, gas + wind together supplied approximately 87.6% of the long-run domestic-generation series.

What did not disappear?

External energy exposure. Public thermal generation became somewhat more efficient, rising from about 45.5% to 49.5%, but its fuel mix became increasingly gas-heavy. Natural gas remained approximately 82.3% import-dependent on the project's origin measure in 2025, while direct electricity imports also became more important between 2023 and 2025.

What does this mean operationally?

Ireland is increasingly organised around:

  1. variable indigenous renewables, especially wind;
  2. gas-fired flexibility;
  3. interconnection / electricity imports;
  4. storage, which remains small in annual energy terms but can matter during specific hours.

The monthly evidence does not support a simplistic one-for-one rule that low wind automatically means high gas. Imports, demand, storage, outages, prices and other generation also matter.

What does this mean economically?

There is no defensible single cost league table that makes gas, wind and solar directly equivalent.

For gas, fuel price, plant efficiency and EU ETS carbon costs all matter. For wind and solar, capital, financing, grid connection, curtailment and investment risk are central; RESS strike prices are an Irish support/revenue benchmark, not a universal LCOE.

At an illustrative 50% gas efficiency, the project's 2025 assumptions produce a direct carbon cost of roughly €29.9/MWh of electricity before the gas itself is purchased.

Final interpretation: Ireland's transition has reduced coal and peat dependence and dramatically expanded renewable generation, but it has not created a self-sufficient electricity system. Ireland is moving toward a system that combines renewable resources with imported gas, interconnection and storage. The core challenge is balancing decarbonisation, flexibility, investment cost, fuel-price exposure and security of supply.
