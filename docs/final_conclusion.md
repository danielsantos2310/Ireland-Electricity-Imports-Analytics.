# Final conclusion

## What changed?

Between 2010 and 2025, Ireland's long-run domestic generation series increased only from **26.63 TWh to 29.31 TWh**.

The scale changed modestly. The **composition changed radically**.

Wind's share rose from **10.5% to 40.1%**, while gas fell from **64.4% to 47.5%** and coal fell from **13.0% to 1.5%**.

By 2025, gas + wind together supplied approximately **87.6%** of the long-run domestic-generation series.

## What did not disappear?

External energy exposure.

Public thermal generation became somewhat more efficient, rising from about **45.5% to 49.5%**, but its fuel mix became increasingly gas-heavy.

Natural gas remained approximately **82.3% import-dependent** on the project's origin measure in 2025.

Direct electricity imports also became more important between 2023 and 2025.

## What does this mean operationally?

Ireland is increasingly organised around:

1. **variable indigenous renewables**, especially wind;
2. **gas-fired flexibility**;
3. **interconnection / electricity imports**;
4. **storage**, which remains small in annual energy terms but can matter during specific hours.

The monthly evidence does not support a simplistic one-for-one rule that low wind automatically means high gas. Imports, demand, storage, outages, prices and other generation also matter.

## What does this mean economically?

There is no defensible single cost league table that makes gas, wind and solar directly equivalent.

For gas:
- fuel cost changes with wholesale gas prices;
- efficiency changes the quantity of gas required per MWh;
- EU ETS creates an additional direct carbon cost.

For wind and solar:
- fuel cost is not the central issue;
- capital, financing, connection, curtailment and investment risk matter;
- RESS strike prices provide an Irish support/revenue benchmark, not a universal LCOE.

At an illustrative 50% gas efficiency, the project's 2025 assumptions produce a direct carbon cost of roughly **€29.9/MWh of electricity** before the gas itself is purchased.

## Final interpretation

Ireland's transition has reduced coal and peat dependence and dramatically expanded renewable generation.

But it has **not** created a self-sufficient electricity system.

Instead, Ireland is moving toward a system that combines renewable resources with imported gas, interconnection and storage. The core challenge is therefore not merely choosing the nominally cheapest technology: it is balancing **decarbonisation, flexibility, investment cost, fuel-price exposure and security of supply**.

That conclusion provides the evidence base for **Project 05 — Ireland Electricity Imports Analytics** and ultimately **Project 06 — Produce vs Import Analytics**.
